How to get more Google reviews without breaking the rules

Ask anyone how they chose a new dentist, plumber or restaurant and you'll hear the same thing: "It had good reviews." Reviews are one of the main things people check before calling, and they also help you rank in Google Maps. The good news is that most happy customers are willing to leave one. They just need to be asked at the right moment, with as little effort as possible.

Why reviews matter so much

  • They push you up in local search and the map pack.
  • They turn a search result into a phone call: a 4.8 with 120 reviews beats a 5.0 with 3.
  • Recent reviews show the business is active. A great rating from two years ago doesn't reassure anyone.
  • They tell you what customers actually value, in their own words.

Ask at the moment of peak happiness

Timing is everything. The best moments are:

  • right after a job is finished and the customer is visibly pleased;
  • when they thank you or compliment the work;
  • a day after delivery, once they have used the product;
  • after a successful appointment, before they leave or the same evening.

Asking weeks later rarely works. People forget, and the good feeling fades.

Make it take ten seconds

Every extra step loses people. Get your direct review link from your Google Business Profile (the "Ask for reviews" or "Share review form" option) and put it everywhere:

  • a short text or WhatsApp message after the visit;
  • the thank-you email or invoice;
  • a QR code at the counter, on the receipt or on a small card in the package;
  • your email signature.

Message templates you can copy

Hi Anna, thanks again for coming in today! If you were happy with your visit, a quick Google review would mean a lot to our small team: [link]. It takes about 30 seconds.
Thanks for choosing us for the kitchen repair, Mark. If you have a minute, would you mind sharing your experience on Google? [link] It really helps other people find us.

Short, personal, with one link. That's it.

Reply to every review

Replies show future customers that real people run the business.

  • Positive reviews: thank them by name and mention something specific ("glad the cake was a hit at the party").
  • Negative reviews: stay calm, apologise for the experience, take it offline ("please call us at…"), and fix what you can. A thoughtful reply to a bad review often wins more trust than ten five-star ones.
  • Reply within a few days, not months.

What not to do

Some shortcuts can get reviews removed or the whole profile penalised, and in many countries they're illegal:

  • Don't buy reviews or post fake ones, including from friends and staff pretending to be customers.
  • Don't pay or give discounts for reviews. Google's policy forbids incentives, and consumer-protection rules in the US, UK and EU are tightening against them.
  • Don't "gate" reviews by only sending the link to people who said they were happy. Ask everyone the same way.
  • Don't review competitors or ask others to.

Slow and honest wins here. A steady flow of real reviews beats a sudden burst that looks suspicious.

Build it into your routine

The businesses with hundreds of reviews don't have magic. They have a habit:

  1. Every finished job or visit triggers a thank-you message with the review link.
  2. Someone checks new reviews twice a week and replies.
  3. Once a month you read them all and note what people praise and complain about.

You can automate step 1 with your booking system or a simple chatbot or messenger flow, so nobody has to remember.
