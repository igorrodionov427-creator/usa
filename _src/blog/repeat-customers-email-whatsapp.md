Winning a new customer is expensive. Getting a past customer to come back is usually much cheaper, and yet most small businesses never write to their customers again after the first order. Their list sits in a spreadsheet, a POS system or a phone's contacts, doing nothing.

Here's how to turn that list into steady repeat sales, without becoming the brand people unsubscribe from.

## Build a list you're allowed to use

Rules differ by country, and they matter:

- In the **EU and UK**, marketing emails and messages to individuals generally need consent: a ticked box, a sign-up form, or an existing customer who was given a clear chance to opt out when they bought (rules vary by country).
- In the **US**, CAN-SPAM allows marketing emails but requires an honest sender, your physical address and a working unsubscribe link. Texts and WhatsApp messages have stricter consent rules.
- **WhatsApp** itself requires that people opt in to receive your business messages, and marketing messages go through approved templates.

Simple ways to collect consent: a checkbox at checkout, a QR code at the counter ("get our offers on WhatsApp"), a short form on your site, or a question at the end of a booking. This isn't legal advice; check what applies in your market.

## Email or WhatsApp?

- **Email** is cheap, good for longer content (new collections, seasonal guides, newsletters) and works everywhere.
- **WhatsApp** gets read far more often in many countries and feels personal, which makes it great for reminders, quick offers and order updates. Marketing messages are paid per message, so use it for things that matter.

Most businesses do best with both: email for regular updates, WhatsApp for timely, high-value messages.

## Split your list into a few groups

You don't need complex segmentation. Three to five groups go a long way:

- **New customers** (first order in the last 30 days);
- **Regulars** (several orders);
- **Sleeping customers** (no order in 3–6 months);
- by **interest or product**, if what you sell varies a lot.

A message aimed at a group always beats the same blast to everyone.

## Messages that bring people back

- **Thank you + tip** a few days after the first purchase: how to use or care for what they bought.
- **Reminder** when it's time to come back: a haircut after six weeks, a dental check-up after six months, a refill when the product runs out.
- **"We miss you"** for sleeping customers, with a simple reason to return.
- **First access** to something new, for regulars.
- **Seasonal** messages tied to holidays and local events.
- **Review request** after a good experience (see [how to get more Google reviews](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews/)).

Keep each one short, with one clear action. People forgive a brand that writes rarely and usefully.

## Automate the routine ones

The highest-performing messages are often the automatic ones, because they arrive at exactly the right time:

1. welcome message when someone joins;
2. thank-you and review request after an order;
3. reminder when the next visit or refill is due;
4. win-back after a few months of silence.

Set them up once and they work every day. A booking system, an email tool or a small [messenger bot](/blog/chatbot-for-small-business/) can handle them.

## How often to send

For most small businesses: one or two emails a month and occasional WhatsApp messages only when there's something genuinely useful. If unsubscribes jump, you're sending too often or too broadly.

## What to measure

- **Repeat purchase rate:** what share of customers buy again.
- **Revenue from each campaign or automation,** using a code, link or "where did you hear about it?" question.
- **Open and reply rates** as early signals (email opens are less reliable now because of privacy features).
- **Unsubscribes and blocks** as your warning light.
